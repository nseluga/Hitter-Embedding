\section*{Introduction}
A hitter's platoon split takes several hundred plate appearances against each pitcher hand to stabilize, and teams need the number long before that sample exists. The standard answer is to shrink the observed split toward a league or handedness average, which discards the pitch-level record of how the hitter got there. Prior player embeddings learn from at-bat outcomes or pitch sequences, but they are read out for recent form or game-level predictions; none is composed into a season split a team would project, or scored against how much of that split the data can support. We train a hitter model on pitches and query it for the split.

\section*{Methods}
The data are 7.3 million Statcast pitches from 2015 through 2024 covering 1,870 hitters. A network of under one million parameters reads a 32-dimensional hitter embedding and the pitch context and predicts, in sequence, whether the hitter swings, whether contact follows, and the exit velocity, launch angle, and spray direction of the ball in play, each as quantile bins. A platoon split is then a query: we average the model over every pitcher in the training seasons weighted by batters faced, compose the pitch outcomes through the count-state Markov chain exactly to wOBA, and average five seeds. Evaluation is walk-forward, with 2025 sealed and scored once against a pre-registered gate: beat a gradient-boosted and an empirical Bayes baseline on both error and rank, reported by prior plate appearances against left-handers. The measurement ceiling is the correlation an error-free predictor could reach given the reliability of one season's side-specific wOBA.

\section*{Results}
The pre-registered gate did not pass. Pooled error did not separate from either baseline, while pooled rank correlation beat both. Against the ceiling, the model recovers 55 percent of the platoon signal the data can support and 63 percent of the overall level signal (Figure 1). By stratum (Table 1), it beats both baselines on error and rank only for hitters with 113 to 452 prior appearances. Below 113 it is worse than both; 43 percent of those hitters never appeared in training, and the stratum's calibration slope is 0.45, so its predictions are too spread out rather than misordered. Within a handedness the model predicts the direction of a hitter's split at 0.565 against a permutation null of 0.499.

\section*{Conclusion}
A process model with no split-specific training recovers about half the platoon skill one season can measure. The embedding, trained only on pitch outcomes, separates hitters on power, contact, and plate discipline at 0.75 to 0.80 held-out accuracy. The remaining gap is among hitters the model has barely seen, and it is a calibration problem. A cold-start prior for that stratum is the next step.
